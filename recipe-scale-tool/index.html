Grams to Cups Conversion Guide

Converting grams to cups is one of the most common challenges in cooking and baking. Unlike liquid measurements, dry ingredients can't be converted with a single formula — density matters. One cup of flour weighs about 120–130 g, while one cup of sugar weighs around 200 g. Using a recipe converter removes the guesswork entirely.

Why Grams Are More Accurate Than Cups

Cups measure volume, not weight. When you scoop flour with a measuring cup, you can pack anywhere from 100 g to 160 g depending on your technique. Professional bakers always weigh ingredients for consistency. That said, most home recipes list cups — which is exactly why conversion tools matter.

How to Scale Recipes Easily

Scaling a recipe — whether doubling it for a crowd or halving it for one — sounds simple, but certain ingredients don't scale linearly. Understanding which ingredients to scale directly, and which require adjustment, is key to consistent results.

The Simple Rule: Most Ingredients Scale Linearly

Flour, sugar, butter, eggs (approximately), vegetables, and proteins all scale proportionally. If a recipe feeds 4 and you want to feed 8, multiply every ingredient by 2. Our recipe scaler above handles this automatically — enter your original and target servings and every ingredient updates instantly.

Ingredients That Don't Scale 1:1

  • Salt & spices: Scale at 75% when doubling. Strong flavors intensify non-linearly.
  • Leavening agents (baking powder, yeast): Scale at 60–75% when tripling or more.
  • Alcohol & vinegar: Use 50–70% when doubling — acidity gets pronounced.
  • Gelatin: Scale exactly — this one actually does work linearly.

Scaling Baking vs. Cooking

Cooking (soups, stews, sautés) is forgiving. Baking is chemistry. When scaling baked goods beyond 2×, bake times and temperatures may need adjustment — a doubled cake batter often takes only 15–20% longer to bake, not twice as long. Always use a thermometer to check doneness.

Common Cooking Measurement Mistakes

Even experienced cooks make measurement mistakes that silently ruin recipes. Here are the most common errors — and how to avoid them using a reliable recipe converter and good technique.

1. Scooping Flour Directly from the Bag

This packs air out of the flour, resulting in up to 30% more flour than intended. The correct method: spoon flour into the measuring cup and level off with a straight edge. Better yet, weigh it — 125 g per cup, every time.

2. Confusing Fluid Ounces and Weight Ounces

8 fl oz of water equals 8 oz by weight (237 g). But 8 fl oz of honey weighs about 12 oz (340 g). Fluid ounces measure volume; weight ounces measure mass. When a recipe says "8 oz," context (liquid vs. solid) determines which it means. Use our ingredient calculator to avoid confusion.

3. Not Accounting for Ingredient Temperature

Cold butter measures differently than room-temperature butter. For baking, weight measurements eliminate this entirely. "1 cup of butter" is ambiguous; "225 g of butter" is precise regardless of temperature or how it's cut.

4. Using Metric and Imperial Interchangeably

  • 1 US cup = 240 ml — but 1 UK/Australian cup = 250 ml
  • 1 US tablespoon = 14.8 ml — but 1 Australian tbsp = 20 ml
  • When following international recipes, always check which system is used

5. Eyeballing Small Amounts

The difference between ¼ tsp and ½ tsp of baking soda in a cake can mean the difference between a perfect rise and a flat, dense result. For amounts under 1 tsp, use a proper measuring spoon — never estimate.
